## 1. Bolus vs. spread intake

- A 1 L water bolus drunk over 30 min by 72 euhydrated men produced **1337 ± 330 g of urine over the following 4 h** — i.e. most of the bolus was excreted, not retained. Beverages with electrolyte/energy content (ORS 1038 g, skim milk 1049 g, full-fat milk 1052 g, all p<0.001 vs water) were retained substantially better, which is the entire basis of the Beverage Hydration Index (Maughan 2016, Am J Clin Nutr — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26702122/)†

- Direct head-to-head of pattern: after 2% body-mass exercise dehydration, the same water volume given as a **bolus (100% in 1 h)** vs **metered (12.5% every 30 min over 4 h)** gave urine outputs of **700 mL vs 420 mL** and hydration efficiency of **55% vs 75% (p = 0.018)**. This is the single cleanest "distribution matters" experiment I found (Jones 2010, Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20479487/)†

- Replicated with a carbohydrate-protein beverage at 125% of losses: bolus (60 min) vs metered (4.5 h) gave **1129 ± 164 mL vs 865 ± 190 mL urine (p = 0.003)** and hydration efficiency **53.7% vs 69.1% (p = 0.004)** (McBride 2020, Int J Exerc Sci — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7745904/)†

- **Mechanism is confirmed, not assumed.** An oral water load in 20 healthy volunteers dropped median plasma osmolality **290 → 281 mOsm/kg** and median plasma copeptin **3.3 → 2.0 pmol/L**, with copeptin/AVP correlating at r ≈ 0.8 across hypo-, iso- and hyperosmolar states. Rapid plasma dilution → AVP suppression is measurable within a single drinking bout (Balanescu 2011, J Clin Endocrinol Metab — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21289257/)†

- **Important contrary result:** with a carbohydrate-electrolyte solution at 120% of losses, ingesting 60/40/20% over 3 h vs 24%/h over 5 h produced **no significant difference in 6-h urine output**; hydration efficiency differed by only 3 ± 5%. The bolus penalty appears to be largest for plain, low-osmolality water and attenuated when the drink carries solute (Kovacs 2002, Eur J Appl Physiol / Int J Sport Nutr — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11993619/)‡

- Kinetics bound the useful window: ingested D₂O-labelled water (300 mL) appeared in plasma within **~5 min**, absorption half-life **~11–13 min**, essentially complete absorption by **~75–120 min** (Péronnet 2012, Eur J Appl Physiol — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21997675/)‡

## 2. Time-course of dehydration between drinks

- The best-resolved human time-course: 15 adults, total fluid restriction starting in the evening. Serum osmolality **281 → ~287 mOsm/kg by 13 h** (already p<0.001 vs euhydration), **~291 at 24 h**, then **flat 24→37 h** ("reached a maximum at 24 h and did not increase further"). Body-mass loss was **~1.0% at 13 h, 1.9 ± 0.4% at 24 h, 2.7 ± 0.6% at 37 h** (Shirreffs 2004, Br J Nutr 91:951–958 — https://sure.sunderland.ac.uk/id/eprint/5057/1/04082014.pdf)†

- In the same study, **thirst rose over 0–13 h and then did not increase further (P>0.05)**, and thirst correlated poorly with actual deficit (R² = 0.11 vs % body-mass loss; R² = 0.09 vs plasma volume change). Thirst is an early, saturating, badly-calibrated signal — it tells you a gap happened, not how big it is (Shirreffs 2004, Br J Nutr — https://sure.sunderland.ac.uk/id/eprint/5057/1/04082014.pdf)†

- Pooled osmotic thirst threshold across studies is **285.23 ± 1.29 mOsm/kg**, with thirst rising linearly above it (slope 0.54 ± 0.07 cm/mOsm/kg, mean r = 0.91). Note this is *below* the commonly quoted 290–295 figure (Hughes 2018, Perioperative Medicine — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5763530/)†

- **Plasma osmolality is a poor within-day signal in free-living people.** In a 5-day inpatient crossover at 1.0 vs 2.5 L/day, plasma and saliva osmolality did **not** differ between intake levels (p = 0.82 / 0.16); only urine concentration and volume responded, and they stabilised within ~24 h of an intake change (Perrier 2013, Eur J Appl Physiol — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3714557/)†

- Circadian shape of the day: **overnight and first-morning urine are the most concentrated; afternoon urine is the least concentrated**, and late-afternoon (16:00–20:00) samples best track the 24-h value (87% within 50 mOsm/kg). The overnight gap is the reliably worst part of the day (Perrier 2013, Eur J Appl Physiol — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3714557/)†

- **No study I found tests a specific maximum inter-drink interval.** The 2h/3h/4h figures used by apps have no direct experimental basis. The nearest anchor is that ~13 h without fluid (overnight) already moves serum osmolality ~+6 mOsm/kg.

## 3. Symptoms of mild hypohydration

- Women, 25 subjects, **1.36 ± 0.16% body-mass deficit**: significant adverse effects on vigor-activity, fatigue-inertia and total mood disturbance at rest and during exercise, plus **headache symptoms, lower concentration and increased perceived task difficulty**. Critically, "most aspects of cognitive performance were not affected" (Armstrong 2012, J Nutr 142:382–388 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22190027/)†

- Men, 26 subjects, **1.59 ± 0.42% deficit** (exercise + furosemide): increased visual vigilance errors (p = 0.048) and slower working-memory latency (p = 0.021); **no** effect on psychomotor vigilance, four-choice RT, repeated acquisition or grammatical reasoning. Fatigue/inertia ↑ (p = 0.040 rest, 0.026 exercise), tension/anxiety ↑ (p = 0.029) (Ganio 2011, Br J Nutr 106:1535–1543 — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/mild-dehydration-impairs-cognitive-performance-and-mood-of-men/3388AB36B8DF73E844C9AD19271A75BF)†

- Dry mouth is the earliest and most consistent symptom: mouth-dryness ratings were significantly elevated at **every** fluid-restriction timepoint from 13 h onward, and mouth dryness correlated with thirst at P<0.001 (R² = 0.75). Headache only became significant at **24 h (P<0.005)** and 37 h, not at 13 h (Shirreffs 2004, Br J Nutr — https://sure.sunderland.ac.uk/id/eprint/5057/1/04082014.pdf)†

- Meta-analysis of 33 studies / 280 effect sizes / 413 subjects at 1–6% BML: overall cognitive impairment is **small but real (ES = −0.21, 95% CI −0.31 to −0.11)**; attention −0.52, executive function −0.24, motor coordination −0.40, reaction time n.s. Effect at **≤2% BML was ES −0.14** vs −0.28 above 2% (Wittbrodt & Millard-Stafford 2018, Med Sci Sports Exerc — https://www.ovid.com/jnls/acsm-msse/pdf/10.1249/mss.0000000000001682~dehydration-impairs-cognitive-performance-a-meta-analysis)‡

- Headache RCT: 102 recurrent-headache patients randomised to +1.5 L water/day improved **Migraine-Specific QoL by 4.5 points (95% CI 1.3–7.8)** and 47% vs 25% reported meaningful improvement — but there was **no change in days with at least moderate headache**. Subjective benefit, no objective headache reduction, unblinded (Spigt 2012, Family Practice 29:370–375 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22113647/)‡

## 4. Vasopressin / copeptin and low-grade "hydration debt"

- Copeptin tracks acute drinking within a single bout (3.3 → 2.0 pmol/L after one water load), so a spiky pattern *does* mechanically imply oscillating AVP — the biomarker responds on the timescale of hours, not days (Balanescu 2011, J Clin Endocrinol Metab — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21289257/)†

- Prospective community cohort (D.E.S.I.R., n = 1234, 9 y): plasma copeptin independently predicted progression to CKD, **HR 1.65 per log₁₀ copeptin (95% CI 1.06–2.54, p = 0.02)**; OR 3.03 (1.21–7.57) for KDIGO-defined renal function decline (Roussel 2015, Am J Nephrol 42:107–114 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26346607/)†

- Copeptin is modifiable by drinking more: +1.5 L/day for 6 weeks lowered median copeptin **12.9 → 7.8 pmol/L (p<0.001)** and fasting glucose 5.94 → 5.74 mmol/L (p = 0.04), with the effect concentrated in the highest baseline-copeptin tertile. Notably, **no timing or distribution instructions were given** — only total volume (Enhörning 2019, J Clin Endocrinol Metab, H2O Metabolism pilot — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6541888/)†

- Observational support: in 2148 community adults over 6 y, 24-h urine volume ≥3 L/d vs 1–1.9 L/d had **adjusted OR 0.46 (95% CI 0.23–0.92)** for rapid (≥5%/y) eGFR decline (Clark 2011, CJASN 6:2634–2641 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21885793/)‡

- **The RCT does not support the causal claim.** CKD-WIT randomised 631 stage-3 CKD patients to coached higher water intake vs usual intake; 1-year eGFR change was **−2.2 vs −1.9 mL/min/1.73 m² — no significant difference** (Clark 2018, JAMA — https://www.ovid.com/journals/jama/abstract/10.1001/jama.2018.4930~effect-of-coaching-to-increase-water-intake-on-kidney)‡

- Adjacent epidemiology: middle-age serum sodium >142 mmol/L was associated with **39% higher risk of chronic disease** and >144 mmol/L with 21% higher premature mortality in ARIC (n = 15,752, 25 y). Observational, sodium is a crude hydration proxy (Dmitrieva 2023, eBioMedicine — https://www.thelancet.com/journals/ebiom/article/PIIS2352-3964(22)00586-2/fulltext)‡

- **Nothing I found tests "spiky vs even distribution at matched total volume" against copeptin or kidney outcomes.** Every intervention in this literature manipulates daily total only.

## 5. Existing metrics — rolling windows, drinking frequency

- **There is no published rolling-window hydration metric.** I searched specifically for trailing-N-hour intake indices, drinking-frequency scores and longest-gap metrics; the only hits were patents and consumer calculators, not peer-reviewed work (search executed; nearest published construct is the fixed 2-h post-ingestion window of the BHI, which is a beverage-comparison assay, not a person-level metric — Maughan 2016, Am J Clin Nutr — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26702122/)†

- The largest harmonised drinking-behaviour dataset (Liq.In₇, n = 10,521, 6 countries, 7-day event-level fluid records) captured every drinking event with volume, type, location and food co-occurrence — and still **reported no per-day count of drinking occasions and no gap statistics**. It categorised behaviour only qualitatively ("with meals", "standalone", "grazing"); fluid taken with meals ranged 26–48% of total by country (Morin 2018, Eur J Nutr — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6008358/)†

- A study explicitly titled around "drinking patterns and hydration biomarkers" (n = 156, 7-day records) in practice analysed only **total volume and beverage type** against urine osmolality — it did not analyse frequency, timing or distribution at all (Zhang 2020, BMC Public Health — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7140363)†

- The standing clinical/regulatory metric is a **24-h total**, full stop: EFSA Adequate Intake 2.0 L/day (women) and 2.5 L/day (men) for total water, with no distribution guidance (EFSA NDA Panel 2010, EFSA Journal 8:1459 — https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/1459)‡

- The best-argued adequacy framework in the requested journal list is also volume-based and explicitly permissive: "a wide range of fluid intakes are compatible with euhydration," with total body water varying only **600–900 mL (<1% body mass) day to day** (Cheuvront & Kenefick 2016, J Am Coll Nutr 35:127–134 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26885571/)‡

- App-level evidence exists only for adherence, not physiology: a smart bottle with reminders raised fluid intake **+1.37 L vs +0.79 L** vs advice alone in stone formers (Randomized trial of a smart water bottle, J Endourol/Urology — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1051227621001849)‡

**Verdict: a rolling-window / longest-gap hydration metric is a design invention, not a validated construct.** It is *consistent* with physiology (§1, §2) but has never been operationalised or validated in the literature.

## Confidence

**Well-evidenced**
- Bolus water is retained worse than the same volume spread out, in the acute post-exercise setting: two independent RCTs with concordant effect sizes (~20 percentage points of hydration efficiency) plus the BHI dataset (Jones 2010; McBride 2020; Maughan 2016).
- The mechanism (plasma dilution → AVP/copeptin suppression → diuresis) is directly measured, not inferred (Balanescu 2011).
- Overnight/13 h without fluid produces a real, statistically robust rise in serum osmolality (~+6 mOsm/kg) and ~1% body-mass loss (Shirreffs 2004).
- Dry mouth precedes headache; headache requires ~24 h of restriction (Shirreffs 2004).
- Mood/fatigue effects at ~1.4–1.6% deficit are reproducible in both sexes (Armstrong 2012; Ganio 2011).
- Copeptin is elevated by low intake and lowered by higher intake (Enhörning 2019).

**Weakly evidenced**
- Cognitive impairment at ≤2% deficit: meta-analytic ES −0.14, and the two flagship studies found most cognitive domains *unaffected*.
- Water intake → kidney protection: strong observational signal, null RCT.
- Water intake → fewer headaches: subjective QoL improved, objective headache days did not.
- The bolus penalty for non-water beverages (Kovacs 2002 is null).

**Extrapolation (no direct evidence)**
- Any specific maximum inter-drink gap (2h/3h/4h).
- Any rolling-window trailing-intake metric.
- That an even intake distribution at matched 24-h total improves any health outcome.
- That within-day AVP spikes (as opposed to chronically high mean AVP) carry kidney risk.

## What the evidence does NOT support

1. **"Spread-out drinking improves health outcomes."** Every bolus-vs-metered study measures *fluid retention over 4–6 h in exercise-dehydrated young athletes* (n = 8 and n ≈ 10). None measures a health outcome, none uses a free-living population, none runs longer than a day. Extrapolating a 55%→75% retention difference in a dehydrated cyclist to a seated office worker is unjustified.

2. **"Plasma osmolality tells you when to drink."** It does not move detectably between 1.0 and 2.5 L/day in controlled inpatient conditions (Perrier 2013). It only moves under frank restriction. An app cannot claim to be preventing measurable plasma-level dehydration in a normal day.

3. **"Thirst is a lagging indicator you should get ahead of."** Half-true. Thirst rises early (by 13 h) and then *saturates* — it is a poor magnitude signal but not a late one. The Shirreffs data (R² = 0.11 between thirst and deficit) supports "thirst under-scales", not "thirst arrives too late."

4. **"Drinking more water protects your kidneys."** The only adequately powered RCT is null (CKD-WIT: −2.2 vs −1.9 mL/min/1.73 m²). The copeptin/CKD association is real and dose-graded but observational, and reverse causation (declining kidneys → concentrated urine → higher copeptin) is not excluded.

5. **"Dehydration makes you cognitively impaired."** At the 1–2% levels an app is realistically targeting, the meta-analytic effect is ES −0.14 and both landmark studies reported cognition largely intact. What *is* affected at that level is subjective: mood, fatigue, perceived task difficulty, concentration. An honest app should claim "you'll feel worse," not "you'll think worse."

6. **Population skew.** Almost the entire bolus/spread literature is young, fit, male, exercise-dehydrated, in a heat chamber. Maughan's BHI cohort was 72 males. Jones n = 8. McBride n ≈ 10. None of this was generated in the population a desktop hydration app serves.

7. **The 8×8 / hourly-reminder convention has no source.** EFSA gives a 24-h total and nothing else.

## Design implications

1. **Per-occasion volume cap: ~500 mL.** Above roughly this, marginal retention falls sharply — 1 L over 30 min produced 1337 mL of 4-h urine (net-negative fluid balance) in Maughan 2016, and 100%-in-1-hour bolusing cost ~20 points of hydration efficiency in two RCTs. *[evidence-based for the direction; the exact 500 mL number is a reasonable extrapolation — no study titrated the threshold]*

2. **Hard upper bound per hour: 800 mL, and never log-and-forgive above ~1 L/h.** Maximal renal free-water excretion is ~735–970 mL/h in healthy adults and declines with age and eGFR (Zettl 2024, Int Urol Nephrol — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37794282/ †; Noakes 2001, S Afr Med J — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11732457/ ‡). Sustained intake above this is the hyponatraemia pathway. *[evidence-based]*

3. **Rolling window length: 2 hours.** This is the one window with an experimental anchor — absorption of ingested water is ~complete by 75–120 min (Péronnet 2012 ‡) and BHI is deliberately computed at the 2-h mark (Maughan 2016 †). A trailing-2h intake figure corresponds to "water that has actually landed." *[reasonable extrapolation from evidence-based kinetics]*

4. **Max gap between drinks during waking hours: 3 hours.** There is **no** study on this. What exists: 13 h without fluid produced +6 mOsm/kg and ~1% BM loss (Shirreffs 2004 †). A 3-h waking cap is a conservative fraction of that with no physiological cost, and matches the metered-drinking cadence (every 30 min) that outperformed bolus. Do not present this number as evidence-based. *[reasonable extrapolation]*

5. **Treat the overnight gap as expected, not as failure.** First-morning urine is the most concentrated of the day by design (Perrier 2013 †). Do not penalise the 8-hour sleep gap, and do prompt a drink on wake — that is the one moment with a measurable deficit behind it. *[evidence-based]*

6. **Target ~5–8 drinking occasions across waking hours rather than a fixed hourly ping.** Reaching 2.0–2.5 L/day (EFSA AI ‡) at a ≤500 mL cap arithmetically requires ≥5 occasions; observed real-world behaviour clusters around meals plus standalone/grazing drinks (Morin 2018 †). No study establishes an optimal count. *[reasonable extrapolation]*

7. **Frame symptom messaging around dry mouth and mood, not cognition or headache.** Dry mouth is significant from 13 h; mood/fatigue/concentration at ~1.4% deficit; headache only at 24 h; objective cognition largely intact below 2% (Shirreffs 2004 †; Armstrong 2012 †; Ganio 2011 †; Wittbrodt 2018 ‡). *[evidence-based]*
